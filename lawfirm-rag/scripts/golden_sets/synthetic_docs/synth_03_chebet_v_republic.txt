INTRODUCTION

1. The appellant, Daniel Kiprotich Chebet, was charged before the Chief Magistrate's Court at Eldoret with the offence of robbery with violence contrary to section 296(2) of the Penal Code. The particulars were that on the night of 11 August 2019 at Kapsoya estate in Eldoret, jointly with others not before the court, while armed with a panga and a metal bar, he robbed Ruth Jepkosgei Sang of a mobile phone valued at KES 38,000 and cash of KES 12,500, and at the time of the robbery used actual violence on her.

2. The appellant was convicted and sentenced to twenty (20) years' imprisonment. His first appeal to the High Court was dismissed on 2 May 2023. This is a second appeal, which under section 361(1)(a) of the Criminal Procedure Code is confined to matters of law.

THE PROSECUTION CASE

3. The complainant (PW1) testified that at about 9:30 p.m. she was walking home from a shop when three men emerged from a dark alley. One of them struck her on the left arm with a metal bar while another held a panga to her neck. The attack lasted about two minutes. She testified that there was "some light" from a security bulb on a gate about 20 metres away.

4. She reported the matter at Kapsoya Police Post the same night and was issued with a P3 form. She did not give a description of any of the attackers in her first report. The Occurrence Book entry, which was produced in evidence, recorded "robbed by three unknown men."

5. On 2 September 2019, three weeks after the incident, PW1 saw the appellant at the Eldoret Main Stage and called the police, telling them that he was one of the men who had robbed her. The appellant was arrested at the stage. No identification parade was conducted. PW1 identified the appellant for the first time in court, in the dock, on 14 February 2020.

6. PW2, a clinical officer, produced the P3 form, which classified the injury to the left arm as "harm." PW3, the investigating officer, testified that no stolen item was recovered from the appellant and that his phone records were not obtained.

6A. PW4, Corporal Silas Ruto, the arresting officer, testified that on 2 September 2019 he received a call from the complainant at about 4:15 p.m. and went to the Eldoret Main Stage with a colleague. He found the complainant pointing at a young man in a blue jacket who was standing near a matatu bound for Iten. He arrested the young man, who is the appellant, and searched him, but recovered nothing connected with the robbery. He testified that the appellant was 23 years old at the time and had no previous convictions.

6B. On cross-examination PW4 conceded that the complainant had told him at the stage that she recognised the appellant "by his walking style and his height," and that she did not mention his face. He also conceded that he did not ask the complainant to describe the appellant before pointing him out, and that he did not consider arranging an identification parade because "the complainant was very sure."

6C. The trial was conducted before Hon. E. Wanyama, Senior Principal Magistrate. The record shows that the complainant's evidence in chief and cross-examination were taken on 14 February 2020, that the appellant was unrepresented at the trial, and that the court did not explain to him his right to be represented by an advocate at State expense under Article 50(2)(h) of the Constitution. The appellant has not raised that issue on appeal and we make no finding on it.

THE DEFENCE

7. In a sworn statement, the appellant testified that on 11 August 2019 he was at his workplace, a bakery in Langas, on the night shift from 6 p.m. to 6 a.m. He called his supervisor, DW2, who confirmed the shift and produced a duty roster for that week bearing the appellant's name. The trial court rejected the alibi as an afterthought because it was raised for the first time in the defence.

GROUNDS OF APPEAL

8. The appellant's grounds of appeal, as condensed by his counsel, Mr. J. Barasa, are that the two courts below erred in law:
(a) In relying on the evidence of a single identifying witness made in difficult circumstances without warning themselves of the danger of doing so;
(b) In relying on dock identification without a prior identification parade;
(c) In rejecting the alibi defence without considering whether the prosecution had displaced it; and
(d) In imposing a sentence that was manifestly excessive.

THE JUDGMENTS OF THE TWO COURTS BELOW

7A. In convicting the appellant, the trial magistrate stated that the complainant "impressed the court as a candid and truthful witness who had ample opportunity to see her attackers," and that the light from the security bulb "was sufficient for positive identification." The judgment does not refer to the distance of the bulb from the scene, to the duration of the attack, or to the absence of any description in the first report. The alibi was dismissed in one sentence as "a mere afterthought calculated to escape liability."

7B. On first appeal, the High Court re-evaluated the evidence and found that the complainant "had no reason to lie against a stranger" and that her identification at the bus stage, three weeks after the robbery, "corroborated her evidence in court." The learned Judge did not consider whether the bus-stage identification was itself reliable or whether it could properly corroborate a later dock identification by the same witness. The High Court upheld the sentence of twenty years as "lawful and deserved in view of the violence used."

7C. With respect, honesty and accuracy are not the same thing. A witness who is entirely honest may nevertheless be mistaken, particularly when identifying a stranger seen briefly in difficult conditions. The question is not whether the complainant was truthful, but whether her identification was free from the possibility of error. Neither of the courts below asked that question.

GUIDANCE ON IDENTIFICATION PARADES

7D. Because this appeal turns on the absence of an identification parade, we restate briefly what a properly conducted parade requires. The suspect should be placed among at least eight persons of similar age, height, appearance, and class of life. The suspect should be told of his right to have an advocate or friend present and to choose his position in the line. The witness should not see the suspect before the parade and should be told that the person seen may or may not be present. The officer conducting the parade should not be the investigating officer, and a parade form recording each step and any objection by the suspect should be completed and produced in evidence.

7E. A parade conducted in that manner provides a safeguard against the suggestion inherent in a witness pointing out a person who is already in custody, or in the dock. Where, as here, the witness has already identified the suspect in an uncontrolled setting before arrest, the value of any subsequent parade is diminished, which makes it all the more important that the first identification be scrutinised with care.

SUBMISSIONS

8A. The appeal was heard virtually on 3 February 2026. Mr. Barasa for the appellant relied on written submissions dated 20 January 2026. He argued that the case was a classic example of mistaken identity, that the first identification at a crowded bus stage three weeks after the incident was "highly suggestive," and that identification by "walking style and height," as conceded by PW4, cannot found a conviction for an offence carrying a mandatory minimum sentence of such severity.

8B. Ms. L. Kosgei, Senior Prosecution Counsel, opposed the appeal. She submitted that the second appeal is limited to matters of law; that the two courts below made concurrent findings of fact on identification which this court should not disturb; that the complainant was "very sure" of her identification; and that the alibi was rightly rejected because it was not disclosed during the prosecution case, depriving the prosecution of the opportunity to investigate it.

8C. In reply, Mr. Barasa submitted that the failure of the two courts below to subject the identification evidence to the required scrutiny is itself a point of law, and that a witness's confidence is not a measure of the accuracy of an identification.

Our jurisdiction on a second appeal

8D. On a second appeal this court is confined to matters of law and will not ordinarily interfere with concurrent findings of fact by the two courts below. It will do so, however, where those findings are not based on evidence, are based on a misapprehension of the evidence, or where the courts below are shown to have acted on wrong principles in reaching them. Whether the trial court properly cautioned itself before relying on the evidence of a single identifying witness is a question of law.

ANALYSIS

Identification by a single witness

9. It is settled law that where the case against an accused rests wholly or substantially on the identification evidence of a single witness made under difficult conditions, the court must examine that evidence with the greatest care and warn itself of the danger of convicting on such evidence. The court must consider the length of time the witness had the accused under observation, the distance, the lighting, whether the witness knew the accused before, and any discrepancy between the first description and the accused's actual appearance.

10. In this case the attack lasted about two minutes, at night, with light from a bulb 20 metres away. The complainant did not know the appellant before the incident. Crucially, she gave no description of any attacker in her first report to the police, and the Occurrence Book recorded the attackers as unknown. Neither the trial court nor the first appellate court addressed the absence of a first description or warned itself of the danger of relying on a single identifying witness in these circumstances. That was an error of law.

Dock identification

11. Dock identification is generally worthless unless preceded by a properly conducted identification parade, save in exceptional cases where the witness knew the accused before. Here, the complainant did not know the appellant, and no parade was held. Her first purported identification three weeks later at a busy bus stage was itself unregulated and carried a real risk of mistaken identity. The subsequent identification in the dock added nothing of value. The two courts below erred in law in treating it as reliable.

The alibi

12. An accused person who raises an alibi does not assume any burden of proving it; the burden remains on the prosecution to displace it. While an alibi raised late may be given less weight, it cannot simply be dismissed as an afterthought without evaluation. The appellant's alibi was supported by a witness and a duty roster that the prosecution did not challenge or investigate. The trial court's failure to evaluate this evidence was a further error of law.

12A. As to the prosecution's complaint that the alibi was raised late, we note that the appellant was unrepresented throughout the trial and that nothing on the record shows that he was told he could disclose an alibi earlier. Moreover, the prosecution had the opportunity, after the defence case, to seek leave to call evidence in rebuttal, for example from the bakery's records or the appellant's mobile phone location data. It did neither. The late timing of an alibi goes to its weight, not its admissibility, and it must still be weighed against the whole of the prosecution evidence.

12B. We observe, further, that the complainant's statement to PW4 that she recognised the appellant by his walking style and height is significant. Neither was mentioned in her first report. An identification based on gait and height, by a stranger, at night, three weeks after a two-minute attack, is precisely the kind of evidence that requires the most careful warning before it is acted upon.

13. Having found that the identification evidence was unsafe and the alibi was not displaced, the court need not consider the ground on sentence.

DETERMINATION

14. We are satisfied that the conviction of the appellant was unsafe. The appeal is accordingly allowed. The conviction for robbery with violence is quashed and the sentence of twenty years' imprisonment is set aside. The appellant shall be set at liberty forthwith unless otherwise lawfully held.

15. We make no order as to costs, this being a criminal appeal.
